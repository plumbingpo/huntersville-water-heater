How Davidson Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on descaling once a year if their water is hard.

How old is your water heater? The serial number on the label usually includes the manufacture date, and that date tells you a lot. Most tank heaters reach 8 to 12 years, heat pump units 10 to 15, and well-maintained tankless units around 20. If your heater is past that point and is also showing discolored water, popping sounds, or dampness under the tank, it's usually due for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and less stressful than an emergency replacement after a leak.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to excess pressure or a missing expansion tank, both modest fixes. The same drip left alone for a season can keep stressing the tank until a seam gives way, and a small repair becomes a full replacement plus water damage. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and ruined floors.

Typical Water Heater Issues in Davidson

Around Davidson, water heater trouble tends to trace back to a handful of sources. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be part of the picture: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling you may hear during a heating cycle. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

Then there's age. Once the protective anode rod is gone, usually somewhere in the second half of a tank's life, the tank begins corroding from within, and a slow leak at the base often shows up next. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, so our technicians handle everything from worn thermocouples and sooty burners to tripped high-limit switches and dead elements.

Setup matters too. A home on a private well can see more sediment and occasionally a sulfur smell in the hot water, often fixed by switching the anode rod type. A tankless unit on hard water should get annual descaling. And an older installation getting a new heater often needs code items that weren't required when it went in: an expansion tank, a correctly piped relief valve line, a drain pan, or seismic straps where required. All of these is fixable, and most cost far less when caught early. If something seems off, a diagnostic visit from our team catches the small problem before it becomes an expensive one.
